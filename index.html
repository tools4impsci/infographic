Project: The Activist Infographic

Using Data Visualization for Social Change

Weight: 10% of Final Grade

Project Purpose

This project challenges you to move beyond academic analysis and engage in public persuasion. You will select a social problem and create a compelling infographic designed to inform a specific audience and persuade them to take action. This is an exercise in information design as a form of activism, requiring you to synthesize research, data, and narrative into a single, powerful visual argument.

Learning Objectives

  • Define a clear, persuasive message for a specific target audience.
  • Select and frame data to support a central argument effectively and ethically.
  • Apply principles of visual hierarchy, layout, and information design.
  • Develop a clear and compelling call to action to mobilize an audience.

Infographic Planner

Step 1: Frame Your Message

Distill your entire argument into one powerful sentence.

Who are you trying to persuade?

Step 2: Gather Your Content

List 3-5 key data points that support your core message. For each, choose how you plan to visualize it.

Step 3: Plan Your Visuals

What is the central visual idea? (e.g., a branching tree, a leaking faucet).

List 2-4 primary colors.

Choose a font for headings and one for body text.

Describe how the infographic will be organized from top to bottom.

Step 4: Define the Action

What is the single, clear action you want your audience to take?

Case Study: W.E.B. Du Bois' Data Portraits

Visualizing Data as Activism

In 1900, W.E.B. Du Bois's "Exhibit of American Negroes" in Paris used a series of stunning data visualizations to argue for the progress and humanity of Black Americans. These "data portraits" were a form of activism. By presenting clear, compelling data on economic and social realities, Du Bois created a powerful counter-narrative to the prevailing racism of the era. This is a foundational example of using information design to make a persuasive argument for social change.

Argument 1: Demonstrating Educational Progress

This chart powerfully visualizes the dramatic increase in literacy among Black Americans in Georgia in the decades following emancipation. The thick black bar, representing illiteracy, shrinks dramatically over time. This visualization is a direct rebuttal to racist arguments of the era about intellectual capacity, serving as an undeniable testament to a community's determined pursuit of education against all odds.

Argument 2: Demonstrating Economic Progress

Here, Du Bois counters the narrative that newly freed Black citizens were not contributing economically. This bar chart shows the steady, determined accumulation of property value in Georgia over just 25 years. It's a visual testament to resilience and community-building in the face of immense adversity. The upward trend is an argument for the economic potential and success of Black Americans.

Argument 3: Highlighting Systemic Inequality

This chart directly compares the occupations of Black and White Georgians. By placing the bars side-by-side, Du Bois starkly illustrates economic segregation. This is not a neutral presentation; it is a visual indictment of a system that limits opportunity. Your infographic should similarly use data to build a clear argument.
